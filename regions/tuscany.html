The Centre  /  Toscana

Tuscany

Renaissance cities, cypress-lined crests and Sunday lunches that run until dusk.

Rows of cypress trees zigzagging up a bright green hillside in the Val d'OrciaPhoto: Oishi Kuranosuke, CC BY 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

  1. Home
  2. Regions
  3. Tuscany

Tuscany is where the Renaissance happened, and it still feels lived-in rather than preserved. Florence packs Brunelleschi's dome, the Uffizi and Michelangelo's David into a city you can cross on foot in half an hour, while Siena, Lucca and Pisa each guard their own medieval pride. Between them lie the Chianti hills, striped with vineyards and olive groves, and the bare clay waves of the Crete Senesi rolling south towards the Val d'Orcia.

The real pleasure is in the small things: a glass of Vernaccia on San Gimignano's walls at sunset, a plate of pici in a Montepulciano osteria, the arcaded market square of Figline Valdarno, half an hour up the Arno from Florence. Push past the postcard and you find the Maremma's cattle-herding butteri and free hot springs, the chestnut woods of the Garfagnana, and towns like Pitigliano carved straight out of volcanic tufa.

Capital
Florence
Best time to go
April to early June brings green hills, poppies and easy city walking; September to early November is harvest time, with grape picking, new olive oil, truffle fairs and Autumnia in Figline. July and August are hot, and Florence is at its most crowded.
Getting there and around
Florence Peretola and Pisa Galileo Galilei are the main airports, with Bologna an easy train ride away. Florence Santa Maria Novella is the rail hub, with fast trains to Rome (about 1h30) and Milan (under 2h) and regional lines to Pisa, Lucca, Arezzo and the Valdarno. Siena is easier by bus; for Chianti, the Val d'Orcia and the Maremma you really want a car.

Places to know

Where to spend your days in Tuscany, from the big names to the towns locals choose for a Sunday.

Florence

The cradle of the Renaissance is compact and walkable. Climb Brunelleschi's dome at opening time, book the Uffizi for late afternoon, then cross the Ponte Vecchio into the Oltrarno, where artisans still gild frames and restore furniture around Santo Spirito. End the day at Piazzale Michelangelo or, quieter and higher, on the steps of San Miniato al Monte.

Siena

Siena's shell-shaped Piazza del Campo is one of Europe's great public spaces, best enjoyed simply sitting on its sloping brick. Climb the 400-odd steps of the Torre del Mangia, visit the striped Duomo when its inlaid marble floor is uncovered (usually late summer into autumn), and wander the contrade, each with its own fountain, flag and fierce Palio loyalty.

Val d'Orcia and Pienza

A UNESCO-listed valley of wheat fields, lone cypresses and farmhouses on rounded hills south of Siena. Base yourself in Pienza, Pope Pius II's ideal Renaissance town, where the walls and the lanes called Via dell'Amore and Via del Bacio look straight over the valley, and shops sell pecorino aged in walnut leaves or ash.

San Gimignano

Fourteen medieval towers still stand of the seventy-odd that once crowded this hilltop, raised by rival families trying to outdo each other. Day-trippers thin out by late afternoon, which is when to climb to the Rocca di Montestaffoli, sip a cold Vernaccia di San Gimignano and stay for dinner as the stone turns amber.

Lucca

Lucca kept its Renaissance walls intact and turned them into a tree-lined park: just over four kilometres of promenade you can cycle in twenty minutes. Inside, find the oval Piazza dell'Anfiteatro built over a Roman arena, the holm oaks growing on top of the Torre Guinigi, Puccini's birthplace and buccellato, the town's aniseed-and-raisin sweet bread.

Pisa

Yes, the tower leans, and the Piazza dei Miracoli with its cathedral, baptistery and Camposanto is genuinely astonishing on a clear morning. But stay for the rest: a university city with lively lungarni, the tiny Gothic Santa Maria della Spina on the riverbank, and students eating cecina, the chickpea flatbread, around Borgo Stretto and Piazza delle Vettovaglie.

Things to do

Experiences that only make sense here.

Drive the Chiantigiana

Follow the SR222 from Florence through Greve, Panzano and Castellina towards Siena, stopping at family estates for Chianti Classico tastings and at Dario Cecchini's butcher's shop in Panzano for a meat-heavy lunch.

Soak in Saturnia's free hot springs

At the Cascate del Mulino, sulphurous water at about 37°C pours over travertine terraces below Saturnia, open to everyone at any hour. Go at sunrise on a weekday to have the pools almost to yourself.

Climb Brunelleschi's dome

463 steps lead up between the inner and outer shells of the cupola, past Vasari's Last Judgement frescoes, to a terrace above the rooftops. Book the Brunelleschi Pass well ahead and take the first slot of the day.

Go into the Carrara marble quarries

Take a jeep tour into the quarry basins of the Apuan Alps, where Michelangelo chose his blocks, then eat lardo di Colonnata, cured in marble tubs, in the mountain village of Colonnata itself.

Join the olive harvest

From late October many agriturismi let guests help pick olives, ending at the frantoio where the new oil comes out green and peppery. Toast bread, rub it with garlic, pour the oil over: that is fettunta.

Walk a stage of the Via Francigena

The medieval pilgrim road runs right through Tuscany. The roughly 20 km stage from walled Monteriggioni to Siena crosses woods, farm tracks and quiet hamlets before entering the city through Porta Camollia.

See Florence from the Bardini Garden

In April and early May the wisteria tunnel in the terraced Giardino Bardini is in full bloom, framing one of the finest views of the Duomo. It is far quieter than the Boboli Gardens next door.

What to eat and drink

The flavours of Tuscany, as you would order them at the counter or the table.

Plates

Bistecca alla fiorentinaA thick T-bone, ideally from Chianina cattle, grilled over embers, charred outside and rare within, priced by weight and seasoned with nothing more than salt, pepper and oil.
Pici all'aglioneHand-rolled, fat spaghetti from the Siena and Val di Chiana countryside, tossed in a slow-cooked tomato sauce made with aglione, a giant, mild local garlic.
RibollitaFlorentine 'reboiled' soup of cavolo nero, cannellini beans and stale bread, made a day ahead so it turns thick enough to eat with a fork. Best in winter.
Zolfino beans of the PratomagnoSmall, pale-yellow, thin-skinned beans grown on terraces above the Valdarno near Figline, a Slow Food presidium. Simmered slowly and served with nothing but new olive oil.

Cheese

Pecorino Toscano DOPSheep's milk cheese from the Crete Senesi and Val d'Orcia, sold young and milky or aged under ash and walnut leaves around Pienza. Try it with chestnut honey or a ripe pear.

Cured meats

Lardo di Colonnata IGPPork back fat cured for at least six months with salt, pepper, garlic and rosemary in tubs of Carrara marble. Sliced thin over warm bread, it melts like butter.

Wine

Chianti ClassicoSangiovese-based red from the hills between Florence and Siena, marked with the black rooster: sour cherry, violets and a savoury, food-friendly finish.
Brunello di MontalcinoPure Sangiovese from the slopes around Montalcino, released only five years after the harvest, with at least two years in wood. Deep, firm and built to age.

Oil

Toscano IGP extra virgin olive oilPressed in November, new Tuscan oil is bright green, grassy and peppery enough to catch the back of your throat. Look for the IGP seal and the harvest year.

Sweets

Panforte and ricciarelliSiena's dense, spiced cake of candied fruit, nuts and honey, and its soft, cracked almond biscuits dusted in icing sugar, both Christmas staples that are sold year-round.

Legacy tables

Historic restaurants, cafés and bakeries that have been feeding locals for generations.

More legacy tables across Italy

Caffè Gilli

Florence

Since 1733

Founded as a shop for sweet breads on Via dei Calzaiuoli, on Piazza della Repubblica since the early 1900s, with its marble counter, bronze fittings and stuccoed ceilings intact. Order hot chocolate and a pastry at the bar.

Trattoria Sostanza

Florence

Since 1869

White tiles, shared tables and a narrow room near Santa Maria Novella that Florentines still call 'il Troia'. Order the chicken breast cooked in butter and the artichoke tortino, and book ahead; it closes at weekends.

Buca di Sant'Antonio

Lucca

Since 1782

Already listed as an osteria in a Lucca decree of 1782 and later a post inn, now with copper pans hanging from the beams. Go for farro soup, tordelli lucchesi and roast kid.

Website

Caffè dell'Ussero

Pisa

Since 1775

In the 15th-century Palazzo Agostini on the Arno, this was the café of Pisa's Risorgimento students, Carducci and Giusti among them, and its walls still carry their memories. Have an espresso by the river window.

Caffè Fiaschetteria Italiana

Montalcino

Since 1888

Montalcino's Art Nouveau living room on Piazza del Popolo, created by Ferruccio Biondi Santi, the man credited with Brunello. Red velvet sofas, mirrors, marble tables, and Brunello by the glass.

Stay like a local

Farm stays, village houses and family homes where you live alongside the people who belong here.

How local stays work

Family farm in the Valdarno hills

Above Figline e Incisa Valdarno, small farms like Podere La Casellina, home to the same family since 1936, offer rooms in an old hayloft and invite guests to help with the grape and olive harvests. Florence is a short regional train ride away.

See options

Sleep in a medieval castle-village

Castello di Gargonza, in the hills between Arezzo and Siena, is a whole fortified hamlet whose stone cottages have become rooms and apartments around a tiny piazza and tower. Dante is said to have stopped here in 1302 after his exile from Florence.

See options

Cheese-farm agriturismo near Pienza

Podere Il Casale is an organic sheep and goat farm on the hills outside Pienza where you can watch pecorino being made, take a cheese-making class and eat on a terrace looking out over the Val d'Orcia.

Day trips

Out in the morning, back for dinner.

Day trips from every major city

Florence to Figline e Incisa Valdarno

Walk Figline's arcaded Piazza Marsilio Ficino, see Petrarch's childhood home in Incisa, and in early November catch Autumnia's farmers' market and farm animals in the old centre.

30-40 min by regional train

Florence to Siena

The rapid bus drops you near Piazza Gramsci, a short walk from the Campo. Do the Duomo, the Torre del Mangia and a long lunch, then catch an evening bus back.

About 1h15 by direct bus

Florence to Pisa and Lucca

Pisa's Piazza dei Miracoli first thing, then on to Lucca to cycle the walls in the late-afternoon light before an early dinner and the train home.

About 1h by train to Pisa, then 30 min on to Lucca

Siena to Montalcino, Pienza and Bagno Vignoni

Brunello tasting in Montalcino in the morning, pecorino and a lazy lunch in Pienza, then the steaming thermal square of Bagno Vignoni on the way back.

About 1h by car

Hidden gems

Lesser-known corners that reward the detour.

Hidden gems across Italy

Pitigliano

Rising sheer from a tufa cliff in southern Maremma, Pitigliano was nicknamed 'Little Jerusalem' for its centuries-old Jewish community: the synagogue, kosher oven and ritual bath cut into the rock can be visited. Then walk the Etruscan vie cave, sunken lanes carved deep into the stone.

Figline e Incisa Valdarno

Twin towns on the upper Arno, 30 km from Florence. Figline has a broad porticoed market square named after Marsilio Ficino, the Renaissance philosopher born here; Incisa keeps the house where Petrarch spent his childhood. At festival time look for nana col sedano, duck with celery.

Bagno Vignoni

A Val d'Orcia hamlet whose main square is a stone pool of steaming thermal water; Lorenzo de' Medici and Saint Catherine of Siena came to take the waters. You cannot swim in the pool, but below it, in the Parco dei Mulini, warm channels run over the travertine.

Barga

A hilltop town in the Serchio valley whose Romanesque duomo looks straight at the Apuan Alps. So many Barghigiani emigrated to Scotland that you will hear Scottish accents in the bars, and every summer the town throws a Sagra del Pesce e Patate, a fish-and-chips festival.

Festivals

When to time your visit.

The festival calendar

Palio di Siena

Siena

2 July & 16 August

Ten of the city's seventeen contrade race bareback three times around Piazza del Campo in about 90 seconds, after days of trial heats, processions and long street dinners. Standing in the centre is free.

Event info

Scoppio del Carro

Florence

Easter Sunday

A towering 18th-century cart is hauled by white oxen to the Duomo, where a dove-shaped rocket sent down a wire from the altar sets off its fireworks. A good explosion promises a good harvest.

Autumnia

Figline e Incisa Valdarno

A weekend in early November

Figline's festival of farming, food and the environment fills the old centre with a market of local producers selling oil, wine, cheese and vegetables, a farm of pigs, sheep and cows for children, talks and guided visits.

Event info

Luminara di San Ranieri

Pisa

16 June

On the eve of the patron saint's day, tens of thousands of small oil lamps trace the outlines of the palazzi along the Arno, doubling in the water once the streetlights go off.

Music, craft and custom

Standard Italian grew out of Tuscan, thanks to Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio, yet every town keeps its own accent: Florentines soften their hard c's into the famous gorgia, so 'Coca-Cola' becomes 'Hoha-Hola'. Older traditions survive in ottava rima, improvised sung poetry duels still heard in the Maremma, and in Calcio Storico, a brutal 16th-century ball game played in Piazza Santa Croce every June. The crafts run deep too: Florentine leather and marbled paper, Volterra alabaster, Impruneta terracotta and Carrara marble.
