The Islands  /  Sicilia

Sicily

Greek temples, Arab-Norman gold, a smoking volcano and the best pastry in Italy.

The little island of Isola Bella joined to the shore by a sandbar in a turquoise bay below TaorminaPhoto: Solomonn Levi, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

  1. Home
  2. Regions
  3. Sicily

The Mediterranean's largest island has been Greek, Roman, Arab, Norman, Spanish and Bourbon, and it shows in its stones and on its plates. Doric temples stand in almond orchards at Agrigento, Byzantine mosaics glitter in Palermo and Monreale, and whole towns in the southeast were rebuilt in honey-coloured baroque after the earthquake of 1693. Over it all, Etna smokes and rumbles.

Sicily feels like a small continent. Palermo is chaotic and magnificent, with street markets that smell of fried chickpea fritters and lemons. Catania is black lava stone and loud fish markets. Off the coast lie volcanic Aeolian islands, the Egadi, and Pantelleria, closer to Tunisia than to Palermo. Breakfast is granita with a brioche; dessert might be a cannolo filled to order. Allow more time than you think.

Capital
Palermo
Best time to go
April to June and September to October are best, warm enough to swim from May. Easter brings remarkable processions; July and August are very hot, especially inland, and winters are mild on the coast.
Getting there and around
Fly into Palermo or Catania, with smaller airports at Trapani and Comiso. Trains are slow but useful between the main cities; regional buses fill the gaps, and a car is best for the interior and the southeast. Ferries arrive from Naples, Genoa and Civitavecchia, and hydrofoils serve the Aeolian Islands from Milazzo and the Egadi from Trapani.

Places to know

Where to spend your days in Sicily, from the big names to the towns locals choose for a Sunday.

Palermo

A gloriously messy capital of Arab domes, baroque churches and street food. See the golden mosaics of the Cappella Palatina, the red domes of San Giovanni degli Eremiti and the cathedral, then lose yourself in the Ballarò and Vucciria markets. Take bus 389 up to Monreale cathedral for more mosaics, and the beach at Mondello.

Taormina

A clifftop resort above the Ionian with one of the world's most dramatic theatres: the ancient Greek theatre, with Etna as its backdrop. Walk Corso Umberto, climb to Castelmola for an almond wine, and take the cable car down to swim at Isola Bella.

Mount Etna

Europe's tallest active volcano, at around 3,300 metres. A cable car and 4x4s from Rifugio Sapienza take you close to the summit craters with a guide; lower down, walk through old lava flows and the Silvestri craters, then taste Etna Rosso and Carricante at a winery on the north slope.

Syracuse and Ortigia

Once the greatest city in the Greek world, Syracuse has a vast Greek theatre and the 'Ear of Dionysius' cave in its archaeological park. Its island heart, Ortigia, is a maze of baroque palazzi around a cathedral built into a Greek temple of Athena. Swim off the rocks below the sea walls.

Valley of the Temples

A ridge outside Agrigento lined with Greek temples, including the Temple of Concordia, among the best-preserved Doric temples anywhere. Come at the end of the day, when the stone turns gold, and walk on to the Kolymbethra garden of citrus and almonds below.

Noto and the Val di Noto

Noto was rebuilt after the 1693 earthquake as a stage set of golden baroque, best seen from the steps of San Nicolò cathedral at dusk. Nearby Modica tumbles down a ravine and makes grainy chocolate, and Ragusa Ibla is a hilltop of domes and palazzi. All are UNESCO-listed.

Things to do

Experiences that only make sense here.

Eat your way through Ballarò

Palermo's oldest market is street food heaven: panelle and crocchè in bread, sfincione, boiled octopus and, for the brave, pani ca' meusa, a bun of spleen. Go in the morning when it's busiest.

See an Opera dei Pupi show

Watch knights in tin armour fight Saracens in a puppet theatre in Palermo. The Cuticchio family's Teatro dei Pupi on Via Bara all'Olivella keeps the tradition alive.

Climb Stromboli at sunset

With a licensed guide, hike up the volcano in the late afternoon to watch eruptions from a viewing point at dusk, then head down by torchlight. Book well ahead in summer.

Watch sunset at the Trapani salt pans

Between Trapani and Marsala, windmills stand over shimmering salt pans that turn pink at sunset. Take the little boat to the island of Mozia, a Phoenician town.

Walk the Riserva dello Zingaro

Sicily's first nature reserve: a cliff path of about seven kilometres between San Vito Lo Capo and Scopello, with steps down to pebble coves of very clear water.

Visit Segesta

An unfinished Doric temple alone on a hillside, and a Greek theatre higher up with views to the sea. It is one of the most atmospheric ancient sites in Italy.

Watch a Greek tragedy in Syracuse

Every spring and early summer, the INDA foundation stages classical Greek drama in the ancient theatre of Syracuse, where Aeschylus once premiered plays. Sit on the stone seats as the sun goes down.

Swim at the Vendicari reserve

South of Noto, a protected coast of lagoons with flamingos, a ruined tuna fishery and long sandy beaches such as Calamosche. Bring water and shade: there are no facilities.

What to eat and drink

The flavours of Sicily, as you would order them at the counter or the table.

Plates

ArancineFried rice balls stuffed with meat ragù or ham and mozzarella. In Palermo they're round and feminine (arancina); in Catania, cone-shaped and masculine (arancino).
Pasta alla NormaCatania's classic: pasta with fried aubergine, tomato sauce, basil and grated salted ricotta. Named after Bellini's opera.
Pasta con le sardeA Palermo dish of fresh sardines, wild fennel, pine nuts, raisins and saffron, topped with toasted breadcrumbs. Arab influence in a bowl.

Wine

Nero d'Avola and Etna RossoNero d'Avola gives rich, dark reds in the south; Nerello Mascalese on the slopes of Etna makes elegant, mineral reds that wine critics love.
Marsala and Passito di PantelleriaMarsala is a fortified wine from the west coast; Pantelleria's sweet passito, made from sun-dried Zibibbo grapes, tastes of apricots and honey.

Sweets

CannoloA crisp fried pastry tube filled with sweetened sheep's ricotta, sometimes with candied orange or pistachio. Good shops fill them to order.
Granita with briocheA summer breakfast: almond, lemon, coffee or black mulberry granita with a soft brioche for dipping. Catania and Messina do it best.
Cassata sicilianaA baroque cake of sponge, ricotta cream and marzipan, covered in icing and candied fruit. Traditionally made at Easter.
Pistacchio di Bronte DOPBright green pistachios grown on Etna's lava slopes, harvested every other year and used in pesto, gelato and pastries.
Cioccolato di Modica IGPCold-worked chocolate with a grainy texture from undissolved sugar crystals, made following a method inherited from Spanish rule. Try the chilli or cinnamon versions.

The Assenza family's pastry shop on Corso Vittorio Emanuele, now famous through chef Corrado Assenza. Try the almond granita, the cassata and anything with local citrus or honey.

Stay like a local

Farm stays, village houses and family homes where you live alongside the people who belong here.

How local stays work

A cooking school on a wine estate

Anna Tasca Lanza's school at Case Vecchie, on the Regaleali estate in the hills between Palermo and Caltanissetta, offers stays with classes in the farmhouse kitchen, using produce from the gardens.

See options

A farm stay on the slopes of Etna

Monaci delle Terre Nere, near Zafferana Etnea, is a 19th-century manor surrounded by organic orchards, vineyards and old lava walls, with a restaurant that cooks from the farm and views to the sea.

A dammuso on Pantelleria

Rent a dammuso, the island's traditional domed house of thick black lava stone, with a walled garden built to protect a single orange tree from the wind. Many are run by island families as holiday homes.

A family-run hotel on Salina

The greenest Aeolian island has small hotels run by local families, often among caper fields and vines of Malvasia. Evenings are for granita in Lingua and sunsets over the sea at Pollara.

Hidden gems

Lesser-known corners that reward the detour.

Hidden gems across Italy

Salina

The quietest and greenest of the Aeolian Islands, two extinct volcanoes covered in vines and capers. Watch sunset from Pollara, where Il Postino was filmed, and taste Malvasia delle Lipari at a family winery.

Scicli

A baroque town in a ravine in the southeast, less visited than Noto or Ragusa but just as lovely. It is the setting of the Inspector Montalbano TV series; the town hall is his police station.

Marettimo

The wildest of the Egadi islands, with no cars, mountain trails among wild rosemary and sea caves all around its coast. Local fishermen take visitors round the island by boat, stopping to swim.

Erice

A medieval walled town 750 metres up a mountain above Trapani, often wrapped in mist. Take the cable car up, try the almond pastries at Maria Grammatico, and look down on the salt pans and the Egadi.

Festivals

When to time your visit.

The festival calendar

Festa di Sant'Agata

Catania

3-5 February

One of the biggest religious festivals in the world: hundreds of thousands of people in white tunics pull the saint's silver reliquary through the city for days, with giant candles and fireworks.

U Fistinu di Santa Rosalia

Palermo

14-15 July

A huge triumphal cart carries the saint's statue from the cathedral down the Cassaro to the sea, ending with fireworks over the Foro Italico.

Processione dei Misteri

Trapani

Good Friday

Twenty groups of 18th-century wooden statues depicting the Passion are carried through the streets for about 24 hours by members of the city's ancient guilds.

Infiorata di Noto

Noto

Third weekend of May

Via Corrado Nicolaci is carpeted with huge designs made of flower petals, under the baroque balconies of Palazzo Nicolaci, with a different theme each year.

Music, craft and custom

Sicily's Opera dei Pupi, puppet theatre telling the tales of Charlemagne's paladins, is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list; Palermo's puppets are smaller and lighter, Catania's large and heavy. Painted carts, the carretti siciliani, once carried the same stories on their sides, and cantastorie storytellers sang them in the squares. Caltagirone's 142-step staircase is faced in majolica tiles, and Sicilian, a language of its own, mixes Greek, Arabic, Norman and Spanish words. Holy Week is intense, the marranzano jaw harp twangs at folk festivals, and literature runs from Verga and Pirandello to Lampedusa's The Leopard.
