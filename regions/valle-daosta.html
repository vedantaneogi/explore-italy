The North  /  Valle d'Aosta / Vallée d'Aoste

Valle d'Aosta

Italy's smallest region, where Roman gates sit beneath four-thousand-metre peaks.

Snow-covered Mont Blanc massif rising above golden autumn larches in Val FerretPhoto: Giannico, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

  1. Home
  2. Regions
  3. Valle d'Aosta

Valle d'Aosta is one long glacial valley with a dozen side valleys branching off it, walled in by Mont Blanc, the Matterhorn, Monte Rosa and Gran Paradiso. It is Italy's smallest region and easily its most vertical. You can have breakfast under a Roman arch in Aosta and be out on a glacier terrace at 3,466 metres by lunch. Along the way, dozens of castles and towers still watch over the old road to France and Switzerland.

Officially the valley speaks Italian and French. You'll also hear Francoprovençal patois in the villages, and in the upper Lys valley the Walser still speak a German dialect, a reminder that the Alps were a crossroads long before they became a border. Food follows the cows: Fontina from summer pastures, buttery fonduta, rye bread baked to last the winter. Come in winter to ski, in summer to walk from hut to hut, and in autumn for golden larches and the cow-fighting queens.

Capital
Aosta
Best time to go
Come July to mid-September for the high trails, open rifugi and passes. Late September and October bring golden larches and the final of the Bataille des Reines. Ski season runs December to April, and the Fiera di Sant'Orso fills Aosta at the end of January.
Getting there and around
Turin airport is about 1h30 away by car and Milan Malpensa about 2h. Geneva is roughly an hour from Courmayeur through the Mont Blanc Tunnel. Regional trains and buses link Turin with Aosta via Ivrea (check ahead, since engineering works sometimes swap trains for buses), and local buses run up the side valleys, though a car makes the castles and valley heads much easier to reach.

Places to know

Where to spend your days in Valle d'Aosta, from the big names to the towns locals choose for a Sunday.

Aosta

Rome founded Augusta Praetoria in 25 BC, and its street grid still shapes the centre. Walk from the Arch of Augustus through the double Porta Praetoria to the tall façade of the Roman theatre, then step into the cloister of Sant'Orso to see its carved Romanesque capitals. In the evening, take a glass of Petite Arvine on Piazza Chanoux.

Courmayeur and the Skyway Monte Bianco

Courmayeur's cobbled Via Roma is all wooden shopfronts and après-ski. The real draw is the Skyway, a rotating cable car that climbs from Entrèves to Punta Helbronner at 3,466 metres. From its round terrace, the Dent du Géant, the Grandes Jorasses and the Vallée Blanche glacier fill every direction. Come down afterwards and stroll the flat larch woods of Val Ferret.

Cogne and Gran Paradiso National Park

Italy's first national park started life as a royal hunting reserve, and it saved the Alpine ibex from extinction. Stay in Cogne, beside the wide Prati di Sant'Orso meadow, and walk up Valnontey at dawn, when ibex and chamois graze close to the path. The Paradisia alpine garden is an easy, rewarding half-day.

Breuil-Cervinia

At the head of Valtournenche, the Matterhorn shows its steep Italian face. Cervinia's lifts link with Zermatt for skiing across the border, and on the Plateau Rosa glacier above town some pistes stay open into summer. Just below town is Lago Blu: get there at sunrise, when the peak is mirrored in still water.

Forte di Bard

This huge nineteenth-century fortress plugs the valley's narrowest gorge. The Savoy built it to replace the fort that held up Napoleon for two weeks in 1800. Glass lifts carry you up the rock to the Museo delle Alpi and major photography exhibitions. Below it, the little village of Bard keeps its medieval houses along the old Roman road.

Fénis Castle

This is the most storybook of the valley's castles, with double crenellated walls, square towers and a courtyard painted with saints, Saint George and the dragon, and courtly proverbs in Gothic script. The Challant family built it in the fourteenth century, in a sunny hollow below terraced vineyards. Book a guided tour to see the frescoed upper loggia.

Things to do

Experiences that only make sense here.

Ride the Skyway to Punta Helbronner

Take the first car up from Pontal d'Entrèves while the sky is clearest, then walk out onto the 360-degree terrace above the Vallée Blanche. Bring a warm layer even in August, because it is often below freezing at the top.

Walk a stage of the Alta Via 2

The high route crosses Gran Paradiso country from refuge to refuge. Try the stretch from Valnontey up to Rifugio Vittorio Sella and over the Col Lauson, where you'll see more ibex than people.

Soak in the thermal pools at Pré-Saint-Didier

The outdoor pools at the QC Terme spa steam in the snow, with Mont Blanc straight ahead. Go on a weekday evening, when the crowds thin out and the peaks turn pink.

Taste wine from Europe's highest vineyards

Around Morgex and La Salle, Prié Blanc vines grow on their own roots at up to about 1,200 metres. Visit the Cave Mont Blanc cooperative for a flinty, mountain-fresh Blanc de Morgex et de La Salle.

Follow the castle road

Drive the valley floor from Pont-Saint-Martin's Roman bridge past Bard, Verrès, Issogne and Fénis to Sarre's royal hunting lodge. Check opening days first, because most castles close one day a week and some only open for guided tours.

Ski the Monterosa Ski circuit

Lifts link Champoluc, Gressoney and Alagna across high cols, so you can spend a day crossing three valleys. Off-piste skiers come from all over the Alps for the slopes below Punta Indren.

Join a rifugio dinner

Spend a night at a mountain hut such as Rifugio Walter Bonatti in Val Ferret. The evening means polenta, local cheese and génépy at long shared tables, with the Grandes Jorasses outside the window.

What to eat and drink

The flavours of Valle d'Aosta, as you would order them at the counter or the table.

Plates

Seupa à la vapelenentseValpelline's 'soup' is really a bake: layers of stale rye bread, Savoy cabbage, Fontina and beef broth, cooked until the edges turn crisp and golden.
CarbonadeSalted beef stewed slowly in red wine with onions and warm spices, then spooned over soft polenta. It's mountain food for a cold night.

Cheese

Fontina DOPRaw-milk cheese from Valdostana cows: nutty, supple and at its best in the summer alpeggio versions. Melted with milk and egg yolk, it becomes fonduta.

Cured meats

Lardo di Arnad DOPPork fat cured with herbs and spices in wooden tubs called doils. Slice it paper-thin over warm rye bread with a little chestnut honey.
Jambon de Bosses DOPMountain ham aged at around 1,600 metres in Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses, scented with juniper and mountain herbs and dried by the wind from the Great St Bernard pass.

Wine

Blanc de Morgex et de La SalleA crisp, mineral white made from Prié Blanc vines grown near Mont Blanc on their own roots, untouched by phylloxera.

Coffee & spirits

Caffè alla valdostanaCoffee, grappa, sugar and orange peel, set alight and then passed around in the many-spouted wooden coppa dell'amicizia, so everyone drinks from the same cup.
GénépyA bitter-aromatic liqueur made from alpine wormwood. Ending a rifugio dinner with a small glass is close to obligatory.

Sweets

TegoleThin, crisp almond and hazelnut wafers from Aosta, curved like roof tiles. They are lovely with coffee or dipped into warm zabaglione.

Paolo Griffa al Caffè Nazionale

Aosta

Since 1886

Aosta's grand café on Piazza Chanoux reopened in 2022 under chef Paolo Griffa, who kept its decagonal hall. It now has a Michelin star. Come for pastries by day or the tasting menu at night.

Stay like a local

Farm stays, village houses and family homes where you live alongside the people who belong here.

How local stays work

Sleep at Rifugio Vittorio Sella

This former royal hunting lodge stands at 2,588 metres above Valnontey, in the heart of Gran Paradiso. Hike up in about two and a half hours, eat a family-style dinner and watch ibex wander past the windows at dusk.

See options

Walser village stays in the Lys valley

In Gressoney and Issime, family guesthouses fill old Walser houses built of larch logs on stone bases. Mornings start with rye bread and honey, and Walser footpaths lead from the door to high summer pastures.

Farm stays near the alpeggi

Agriturismi in Valpelline, Valgrisenche and the Gran Paradiso valleys let you watch Fontina being made and eat what the farm produces. Many close in high summer, when the herds go up to the pastures, so book ahead.

Day trips

Out in the morning, back for dinner.

Day trips from every major city

Courmayeur to Chamonix

Cross under Mont Blanc for a day of French mountain town life. In summer you can return on the cable car over the glacier from the Aiguille du Midi to Punta Helbronner.

About 30 min by car or bus through the Mont Blanc Tunnel

Aosta to Great St Bernard Pass

Canons have sheltered travellers at the hospice here for a thousand years. Visit the museum, meet the St Bernard dogs and walk around the cold little lake on the border.

About 50 min by car (road open June to October)

Aosta to Cogne

An easy escape into Gran Paradiso: walk the flat riverside path to Valnontey, then come back for polenta and a cheese board on the meadow.

About 45 min by bus or car

Hidden gems

Lesser-known corners that reward the detour.

Hidden gems across Italy

Lillaz waterfalls

Fifteen minutes' walk from the hamlet of Lillaz, above Cogne, the Urtier river drops about 150 metres in three leaps through larch forest. A loop path climbs beside the falls to a pool where locals cool their feet on hot July afternoons.

Castel Savoia, Gressoney-Saint-Jean

Queen Margherita built this turreted Belle Époque castle as her summer home, facing Monte Rosa. Its rooms are a time capsule of wood and painted ceilings, and the rock garden is full of alpine flowers. The Walser village below has a lakeside path.

Issogne Castle

From outside it looks plain, but Issogne is a Renaissance home rather than a fortress. The arcaded courtyard has a wrought-iron pomegranate fountain and frescoes of a bakery, a tailor's shop and a guardhouse. Together they give you a rare glimpse of daily life around 1500.

Valle di Champorcher

A quiet side valley on the edge of Gran Paradiso, with stone hamlets and a road that climbs to the Dondena plateau. In October its larches turn gold, and an easy walk leads to Lago Miserin and its pilgrimage chapel.

Festivals

When to time your visit.

The festival calendar

Fiera di Sant'Orso

Aosta

30-31 January

This crafts fair, said to be a thousand years old, fills the old town with woodcarvers, weavers and sabot-makers. The night between the two days is the Veillà, when people stay out until dawn with mulled wine and music.

Bataille des Reines

Arena Croix-Noire, Aosta

Spring to autumn; final in October

Pregnant Valdostana cows lock horns in a bloodless shoving match, and the cow that holds her ground becomes queen. Village rounds lead to a packed regional final, with cowbells and plenty of grappa.

Carnevale storico di Verrès

Verrès

Carnival, February

The town re-enacts the story of Caterina di Challant, who, legend says, danced with the townspeople in the square. Expect a Renaissance costume parade, music and a banquet below the cube-shaped castle.

Désarpa

Valleys across the region

Late September

Herds come down from the summer pastures, led by queen cows wearing flowers and huge bells. Villages such as Valgrisenche and Cogne celebrate with markets and polenta.

Music, craft and custom

This valley speaks in layers: Italian and French are both official, the villages talk in Francoprovençal patois, and the Walser of Gressoney and Issime keep a German dialect brought over the passes in the Middle Ages. Woodcarving is the great craft, from the many-spouted friendship cup and the lidded grolla to carved animals and toys, all on show at Sant'Orso. Life still runs on the cattle calendar, with the inalpe in June, the désarpa in autumn and the Bataille des Reines. Old field sports also survive: tsan, rebatta and fiolet, all played with sticks and balls on village meadows.
