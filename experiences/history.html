Experiences  /  History

Three thousand years you can walk through

From Bronze Age towers to the birth of the Republic, Italy keeps its past in plain sight. Here is the story, and where to stand inside each chapter.

Panorama of the Roman Forum with temples, columns and arches below the PalatinePhoto: BeBo86, CC BY-SA 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons

  3. History

In this guide

  1. Italy's story in sixteen chapters
  2. Where history lives
  3. Museums that tell the story

Italy only became one country in 1861, which explains a great deal. For most of its history the peninsula was a crowd of peoples, city-states, kingdoms and foreign possessions, each leaving its own layer: Greek temples in Sicily, Arab domes in Palermo, Byzantine gold in Ravenna, Spanish Baroque in Lecce, Austrian cafés in Trieste. Locals still say they are Florentine, Sardinian or Neapolitan first.

That patchwork is the joy of travelling here. You can eat lunch in a Roman amphitheatre turned into a piazza in Lucca, sleep in a cave house in Matera that was lived in for millennia, and catch a train from the station where Garibaldi's red shirts set off. Use the timeline to put the places in order, then go and find them.

Italy's story in sixteen chapters

Dates are approximate for the early periods. Each entry ends with a place where that chapter still feels close.

c. 1700-500 BC

Nuragic Sardinia

The tower builders

Long before Rome, Bronze Age Sardinians raised some 7,000 stone towers called nuraghi, plus sacred wells and giants' tombs. Experience it at Su Nuraxi di Barumini, then at the Santa Cristina sacred well, a perfect stone stairway descending to water.

Sardinia guide

c. 900-100 BC

The Etruscans

Central Italy's first great civilisation

Masters of metalwork, divination and banqueting, the Etruscans taught early Rome much of what it knew. See their painted tombs at Tarquinia, the mound city of Cerveteri, and the smiling Sarcophagus of the Spouses in Rome's Villa Giulia museum.

Lazio guide

8th-3rd century BC

Magna Graecia

Greek colonies of the south

Greek settlers founded Cumae, Syracuse, Taranto and dozens of other cities, so many that the south was called Great Greece. Stand among the Doric temples of Paestum and Agrigento, or watch a Greek tragedy in Syracuse's ancient theatre.

Campania guide

509-27 BC

The Roman Republic

From city to Mediterranean power

Rome threw out its kings, by tradition in 509 BC, and spent five centuries conquering Italy and then the Mediterranean, building roads like the Via Appia as it went. Walk the Appia Antica's original paving stones south of the city.

Lazio guide

27 BC-AD 476

The Roman Empire

Augustus to the fall of the West

The Colosseum, the Pantheon and Trajan's Market belong here, but ordinary Roman life is best seen at Ostia Antica, Rome's port, where apartment blocks, bars and public latrines survive, and at Pompeii and Herculaneum.

Lazio guide

AD 476-774

Byzantines and Lombards

The early Middle Ages

After Rome fell, Constantinople ruled from Ravenna while the Germanic Lombards took much of the north and centre from 568. Compare Ravenna's golden mosaics with the delicate stucco saints of the Lombard Tempietto in Cividale del Friuli.

Emilia-Romagna guide

827-1194

Arab and Norman Sicily

A meeting of three cultures

Arab emirs brought citrus, sugar cane and irrigation to Sicily; the Normans who followed built churches with Byzantine mosaics and Islamic domes. See the result at Palermo's Palatine Chapel and Monreale Cathedral, listed together as Arab-Norman Palermo.

Sicily guide

c. 1000-1300

Communes and maritime republics

Merchants, towers and fleets

Self-governing towns grew rich and quarrelsome, while Amalfi, Pisa, Genoa and Venice fought over Mediterranean trade. Climb San Gimignano's towers, then compare the striped Romanesque of Pisa's Field of Miracles with the old port of Genoa.

Tuscany guide

1220-1250

Frederick II's southern court

Stupor mundi

The Holy Roman Emperor who spoke several languages, wrote a treatise on falconry and ran a brilliant court in Puglia and Sicily. His octagonal Castel del Monte, alone on its hill near Andria, is still a riddle in stone.

Puglia guide

c. 1400-1550

The Renaissance

Florence and the courts

Bankers like the Medici and princes in Urbino, Mantua and Ferrara paid for a revolution in art and ideas. Stand under Brunelleschi's dome, finished in 1436, then see the ideal Renaissance court in Urbino's Ducal Palace.

Tuscany guide

c. 1550-1750

Counter-Reformation and Baroque

Drama in stone

Rome reinvented itself with Bernini's fountains and Borromini's churches, and after the 1693 earthquake south-east Sicily rebuilt Noto, Ragusa and Modica in honey-coloured Baroque. Lecce carved its own exuberant version from soft local stone.

Sicily guide

c. 1700-1800

The Grand Tour

Europe falls for Italy

Young British and northern European aristocrats came to study the classics; excavations began at Herculaneum in 1738 and Pompeii in 1748, and the Bourbon kings built the vast Royal Palace of Caserta. Follow Goethe to Naples, Paestum and Taormina.

Campania guide

1815-1871

The Risorgimento

Unification

Mazzini dreamed it, Cavour negotiated it and Garibaldi's Thousand invaded Sicily in 1860. The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed on 17 March 1861 in Turin, its first capital, and Rome joined in 1870. Turin's Museo del Risorgimento holds the first Italian parliament chamber.

Piedmont guide

1915-1945

World wars and resistance

A hard half-century

Italy fought on the Alpine front in 1915-18, lived under Fascism from 1922, and was liberated on 25 April 1945 after a brutal war and partisan resistance. Walk the First World War trenches and tunnels of the Lagazuoi above Cortina.

Veneto guide

1946

The Republic

A new country votes

On 2 June 1946, in the first national vote in which women took part, Italians chose a republic over the monarchy. The Constitution took effect on 1 January 1948. Every 2 June, Rome holds a military parade and the Quirinale palace opens its gardens.

Lazio guide

1950s-today

The economic miracle and Europe

Vespas, Fiats and the Treaty of Rome

Postwar Italy boomed into an industrial power and a founding member of the European community, signing the Treaty of Rome on the Capitoline Hill in 1957. Today's Italy is in its design houses, slow-food markets and in every busy piazza at aperitivo time.

Where history lives

Places where the past is not behind glass but all around you.

Ostia Antica

Rome's ancient port

30 min by train from Rome

A whole Roman town of apartment blocks, warehouses, bakeries and a theatre, among umbrella pines and far fewer crowds than Pompeii. It is 30 minutes from central Rome on a regular commuter train, which makes it the easiest half-day escape from the city.

Lazio guide

Tarquinia's painted tombs

Etruscan Lazio

1h30 from Rome

Steps lead down into small chambers painted with dancers, musicians, hunters and a diver plunging into the sea. Afterwards the hilltop town's museum, in a Renaissance palazzo, holds the famous terracotta winged horses.

Lazio guide

Segesta

Western Sicily

1h from Palermo

An unfinished Doric temple alone in a fold of green hills, and a Greek theatre on the summit above, looking out to the Gulf of Castellammare. The Elymians who built Segesta were neither Greek nor Phoenician, and still something of a mystery.

Sicily guide

Aquileia

Friuli-Venezia Giulia

45 min from Trieste

Once one of the largest cities of the Roman Empire, now a village. Its basilica has an enormous 4th-century mosaic floor full of fish, fishermen and Jonah being swallowed by the whale, viewed from glass walkways.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia guide

Cividale del Friuli

Lombard capital

Part of the Longobards in Italy listing

The Lombards' first Italian duchy, perched over the green Natisone river and its Devil's Bridge. Inside the Tempietto Longobardo, six stucco women saints from the 8th century look calmly down from the wall.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia guide

Castel del Monte

Puglia

Built c. 1240

Frederick II's perfect octagon, with eight octagonal towers, crowns a bare hill in the Murge. Nobody quite knows what it was for: hunting lodge, astronomical riddle or statement of imperial power. Come late in the day when the stone turns gold.

Puglia guide

Genoa's old port and palaces

Liguria

Rolli palaces listed 2006

The maritime republic's merchant princes lived on Via Garibaldi in palaces so grand that the city kept a register of them to lodge visiting dignitaries. Below, the carruggi alleys run down to a port Columbus would still half recognise.

Liguria guide

Royal Palace of Caserta

Campania

Begun 1752

The Bourbon kings of Naples wanted their own Versailles and got something bigger: 1,200 rooms and a three-kilometre axis of fountains and cascades. Rent a bike or take the shuttle to the English Garden at the top.

Campania guide

Turin, first capital of Italy

Piedmont

Capital 1861-1865

Elegant arcades, royal residences and the Palazzo Carignano, where the first Italian parliament met. The Risorgimento museum inside tells the story of unification; afterwards, take a bicerin in a café where Cavour once sat.

Piedmont guideMuseo del Risorgimento

Museums that tell the story

Capitoline Museums

Rome

The world's oldest public museum, opened in 1471, with the bronze she-wolf and the original equestrian Marcus Aurelius. The terrace café looks over the Forum.

Lazio guideCapitoline Museums

National Archaeological Museum of Naples

Naples

The frescoes, mosaics, bronzes and everyday objects carried away from Pompeii and Herculaneum, plus the Farnese collection of Roman marbles.

Campania guideMANN

Herculaneum archaeological park

Ercolano

Smaller and more complete than Pompeii, with a beach where hundreds of skeletons were found sheltering in boathouses. An easy stop on the Circumvesuviana train.

Campania guideHerculaneum tickets

Museo Nazionale del Risorgimento

Turin

Paintings, uniforms, Garibaldi's red shirts and the preserved chamber of the first Italian parliament in Palazzo Carignano.

Piedmont guideMuseum site

Valley of the Temples and Pietro Griffo museum

Agrigento

The archaeological museum beside the temples holds a reassembled telamon, one of the giant stone figures that once held up the Temple of Olympian Zeus.

Sicily guidePark and museum

Museo del Violino

Cremona

Five centuries of violin-making in the city of Stradivari, with instruments still played in the auditorium.

Lombardy guideMuseo del Violino

Good to know

  • Buy a good map of the Roman Forum before you go, or join a small-group guide: the ruins make far more sense with someone to read them.
  • Many archaeological parks are exposed and hot; in summer go at opening time with a hat and refillable bottle.
  • Italy's national holidays have historical roots: 25 April (Liberation Day) and 2 June (Republic Day) bring parades, concerts and some closures.
  • Combine sites by train where you can: Ostia from Rome, Herculaneum and Pompeii from Naples, Aquileia by bus from Trieste or Udine.
