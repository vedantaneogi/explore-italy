Experiences  /  Music & dance

The country that invented opera still sings

From Monteverdi's court in Mantua to the all-night pizzica of the Salento, follow Italy's music to the places where it was born and still lives.

Giant Aida stage props lined up beside the Roman Arena in Verona's Piazza BraPhoto: NorbertNagel, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Italy gave the world opera, the piano, the violin as we know it and most of the words musicians still use: forte, piano, allegro, crescendo. In 2023 UNESCO recognised Italian opera singing itself as living heritage. You can sit in the theatres where Verdi's and Puccini's works were first heard, or under the stars in a Roman arena with thousands of others holding candles.

But the music that most Italians grew up with is local. Every region has its dances and instruments: tambourine-driven pizzica in Puglia, the button accordion in Abruzzo, the haunting four-voice canto a tenore of Sardinian shepherds. Summer is when it all comes out, in village squares and on festival stages, often for free and usually until very late.

Where opera was born

Follow the story of Italian opera from a Florentine salon to Puccini's lakeside villa.

Florence and the Camerata

Tuscany, 1580s-1600

Euridice, 1600

A circle of Florentine poets and musicians, meeting at Count Giovanni de' Bardi's palace, set out to revive the sung drama of the ancient Greeks. The result was a new art form. Jacopo Peri's Euridice, first performed at Palazzo Pitti in 1600 for a Medici wedding, is the oldest opera whose music survives. The Maggio Musicale Fiorentino keeps the city's musical flame alive every spring.

Tuscany guideMaggio Musicale Fiorentino

Monteverdi's Mantua

Lombardy

L'Orfeo, 1607

Claudio Monteverdi, born in Cremona, wrote L'Orfeo for the Gonzaga court in Mantua in 1607: the first operatic masterpiece, still performed today. Wander the enormous Ducal Palace, where it was staged, then hear a concert in the jewel-box Teatro Bibiena, where a 13-year-old Mozart played in 1770.

Lombardy guide

Verdi country

Parma and Busseto, Emilia-Romagna

Festival Verdi: Sept-Oct

Giuseppe Verdi was born in 1813 in the hamlet of Le Roncole, near Busseto, among the flat fields and poplars of the Po plain. Visit his birthplace and the little Teatro Verdi in Busseto, see the estate at Sant'Agata where he wrote many of his operas, then come in autumn for the Festival Verdi at Parma's Teatro Regio, where the gallery is famously unforgiving.

Emilia-Romagna guideTeatro Regio & Festival Verdi

Puccini's Lucca and Torre del Lago

Tuscany

Puccini Festival: July-Aug

Giacomo Puccini was born in Lucca in 1858; his family home is now a museum. He wrote La Bohème and Tosca at his villa on Lake Massaciuccoli in Torre del Lago, where he is buried. Every summer the Puccini Festival stages his operas in an open-air theatre on the shore, with the lake as the backdrop.

Tuscany guidePuccini Festival

Rossini's Pesaro

Marche

Rossini Opera Festival: August

Gioachino Rossini, born here in 1792, left his fortune to his hometown and its music school. Each August the Rossini Opera Festival brings top voices to this relaxed Adriatic beach town; you can swim in the morning and hear a rare Rossini comedy at night.

Marche guideRossini Opera Festival

Naples and the canzone napoletana

Campania

Teatro di San Carlo, 1737

Naples gave the world 'O sole mio and Funiculì, funiculà, and a song tradition that runs from 18th-century comic opera to today's street musicians. Hear it in the Galleria Umberto, on a restaurant terrace in Santa Lucia, or at the Teatro di San Carlo, the oldest working opera house in Europe.

Campania guideTeatro di San Carlo

Folk dances and village sounds

Italy's folk music was nearly lost in the 20th century and has roared back. These are the sounds and steps to look for.

Pizzica and the Notte della Taranta

Salento, Puglia

Concertone: late August

Pizzica grew out of tarantismo, a ritual in which women 'bitten by the tarantula' danced for days to frame drums and violins. Today it is a joyful, whirling dance. All summer, villages hold free concerts that build to the giant Notte della Taranta finale in Melpignano each late August.

Puglia guideLa Notte della Taranta

Tarantella

Southern Italy

Weddings and summer feste

The family of fast 6/8 dances from Campania, Calabria and Sicily, played on tambourines, accordions and the chitarra battente. In Calabria it is often a tense, flirtatious duel inside a ring of watching dancers; at weddings, everyone gets pulled in.

Campania guide

Saltarello

Marche, Lazio and Abruzzo

Late summer sagre

The 'little jump' is one of Europe's oldest recorded dances, with tunes surviving from the 14th century. Couples hop and spin to organetto and tambourine at harvest festivals in the central Apennines.

Marche guide

Ballu tundu and launeddas

Sardinia

Patron saints' days

The 'round dance' of Sardinia: a slow, shuffling chain of linked arms that tightens into a circle in the village square, often to the launeddas, a three-pipe reed instrument played with circular breathing. Catch it at the Cavalcata Sarda in Sassari or any patron saint's festa.

Sardinia guide

Canto a tenore

Inland Sardinia

UNESCO-listed 2008

Four men in a tight circle: a solo voice and three throat-sung parts imitating the wind, the sheep and the cattle. UNESCO-listed and still very much part of village life in Barbagia; Orgosolo and Bitti have famous groups.

Sardinia guide

Organetto and zampogna

The Apennines

Castelfidardo, Marche

The little diatonic button accordion drives dancing across central and southern Italy, and Castelfidardo in Le Marche is still the accordion capital of the world. At Christmas, shepherd bagpipers called zampognari walk down from the mountains of Molise and Lazio to play in towns.

Marche guide

Furlana

Friuli and Venice

Friulian folk festivals

A lively couple dance in 6/8 from Friuli that Venetian gondoliers adopted and made fashionable across Europe in the 1600s. Friulian folk groups still dance it in costume at village fairs.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia guide

Festivals worth planning a trip around

Book early for the big opera festivals, especially Verona and Pesaro; the folk and jazz events are often partly free.

Arena di Verona Opera Festival

Verona, Veneto

June-September

Aida, Carmen and Turandot in a 2,000-year-old Roman amphitheatre since 1913. The cheap unreserved stone steps are part of the fun; bring a cushion. The 2027 season runs 11 June to 11 September.

Veneto guideBook the Arena

Puccini Festival

Torre del Lago, Tuscany

July-August

Puccini's operas performed beside the lake where he wrote them, in an open-air theatre near his villa.

Tuscany guideProgramme and tickets

Rossini Opera Festival

Pesaro, Marche

August

Two weeks of Rossini, including rediscovered rarities, in the composer's hometown by the sea.

Marche guideROF tickets

Festival Verdi

Parma and Busseto, Emilia-Romagna

September-October

Verdi's operas in the theatres of his home turf, around his birthday on 10 October.

Emilia-Romagna guideTeatro Regio di Parma

Ravenna Festival

Ravenna, Emilia-Romagna

June-July

Concerts, opera and dance in mosaic-lined basilicas, cloisters and the city's theatres, with big-name orchestras and conductors.

Emilia-Romagna guideRavenna Festival

Umbria Jazz

Perugia, Umbria

July

Ten days of jazz in the medieval hill town, with paid headline gigs in the Arena Santa Giuliana and free concerts and street bands in the squares. A winter edition takes place in Orvieto around New Year.

Umbria guideUmbria Jazz

Maggio Musicale Fiorentino

Florence, Tuscany

Spring

Italy's oldest music festival, founded in 1933, with opera and concerts at the modern Teatro del Maggio.

Tuscany guideTeatro del Maggio

La Notte della Taranta

Salento, Puglia

August

A travelling festival of pizzica through Salento's villages, ending with a vast free concert in Melpignano that runs late into the night.

Puglia guideFestival site

Sanremo Music Festival

Sanremo, Liguria

February

Italy's national song contest since 1951, five nights at the Teatro Ariston each February that the whole country watches and argues about. The winner usually goes on to represent Italy at Eurovision.

Liguria guide

Good to know

  • At the Arena di Verona, unreserved stone-step seats are cheap but hard: rent or bring a cushion and arrive early.
  • Opera houses sell last-minute and gallery seats on the day; ask at the box office rather than giving up on a sold-out show.
  • Village festivals rarely start before 9.30pm in summer. Eat late, as the locals do, and expect the dancing to go on past midnight.
  • Dress codes have relaxed, but at La Scala and La Fenice smart clothes are still appreciated, and shorts are not allowed.
