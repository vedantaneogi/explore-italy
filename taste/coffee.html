Taste  /  Coffee & cafés

Espresso, aperitivo and the art of the bar

How to order like a regular, the coffee rituals that change from city to city, the grand cafés still pouring after two centuries, and the sweet, cold things in between.

The wood-panelled interior of a historic Turin café with marble tablesPhoto: Ivan Ruggiero, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons

In Italy, 'the bar' means the café on the corner, and most people visit it several times a day. Breakfast is a cappuccino and a cornetto taken standing up; mid-morning is a quick espresso with colleagues; evening is a Spritz with salty crisps and olives. The coffee costs about €1.30 at the counter, it's gone in three sips, and it comes with a running commentary on football and politics at no extra charge.

Every city bends the rules a little. Naples pays for a stranger's coffee, Trieste has its own vocabulary, Turin layers chocolate, coffee and cream in a glass, and Venice serves you to an orchestra in Piazza San Marco. Here's how the bar works, a city-by-city guide to Italy's coffee rituals, the aperitivo that follows, and the gelato and granita that fill the hours in between.

How the bar works

Ten seconds of etiquette that will make every coffee in Italy easier.

Stand at the counter, pay the counter price

About €1.30 al banco

Drinking 'al banco' is the norm and it's cheap: an espresso averages around €1.30 nationally in 2026, a little more in the north, less in the south. Sit at a table and you pay for service, which in a grand café on a famous square can mean five times as much. Both prices must be displayed.

In busy bars, pay first

Keep the scontrino

In stations and city-centre bars you often pay at the cassa first, then hand your receipt (scontrino) to the barista and repeat your order. In quieter bars you simply order, drink and pay on the way out.

'Un caffè' is an espresso

Short and strong

Ask for 'un caffè' and you get an espresso. Ristretto is shorter and stronger; lungo is longer; doppio is a double. An Americano is espresso topped with hot water. A caffè macchiato is espresso 'stained' with a spot of milk foam.

Latte means milk

Say 'caffè latte'

Order a 'latte' and you'll get a glass of milk. You want a caffè latte, or more likely a cappuccino. Latte macchiato is hot milk 'stained' with coffee, served in a glass.

Milk in the morning

Before 11am

Cappuccino is a breakfast drink, and many Italians wouldn't touch one after about 11am, least of all after a meal. Nobody will refuse to make you one; you'll just be easy to spot.

Drinks for every hour

Shakerato in summer

Try a caffè corretto, 'corrected' with grappa or sambuca, after a heavy lunch; a shakerato, espresso shaken with ice and sugar, in summer; a marocchino with cocoa and foam in Piedmont; or caffè d'orzo, barley 'coffee', if you're off caffeine.

A glass of water comes free

Drink it before

Many bars serve a small glass of water with espresso, especially in the south, to cleanse the palate. Drink it first. In Naples, if it isn't offered, it's fine to ask.

Don't linger at the bar

Three sips and go

Counter coffee is fast. Drink, chat, say 'grazie, buona giornata', and make room. If you want to sit for an hour with a book, take a table and enjoy it: that's what you're paying for.

A coffee map of Italy

City by city, the rituals and historic cafés worth going out of your way for.

Naples: caffè sospeso and Gambrinus

Campania

Gran Caffè Gambrinus, since 1860

Neapolitan coffee is dense, sweet and served scalding in a pre-warmed cup. The city's kindest custom is the caffè sospeso, the 'suspended coffee': pay for two, drink one, and the second waits for someone who can't afford it. Gran Caffè Gambrinus, a gilded Belle Époque salon on Piazza del Plebiscito since 1860, keeps the tradition alive.

Campania guide

Trieste: a language of its own

Friuli-Venezia Giulia

Order a 'capo in b'

Italy's coffee port, home of illy and old Habsburg coffee houses, speaks a code. A 'nero' is an espresso, a 'capo' is an espresso with a little foamed milk, a 'capo in b' is the same in a small glass (bicchiere), and a 'goccia' gets just a drop of foam. Order one at the Antico Caffè San Marco, a 1914 Viennese-style hall that doubles as a bookshop, or at Caffè degli Specchi on Piazza Unità.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia guideAntico Caffè San Marco

Turin: bicerin and chocolate

Piedmont

Caffè Al Bicerin, since 1763

The bicerin is Turin in a glass: hot chocolate, espresso and cold cream in three layers, never stirred. It was born at Caffè Al Bicerin, a tiny wood-panelled room opposite the Consolata sanctuary, open since 1763. Turin is also the city of gianduja and of the grand arcaded cafés, Baratti & Milano and Mulassano among them.

Piedmont guideCaffè Al Bicerin

Venice: an orchestra on the square

Veneto

Caffè Florian, since 1720

Caffè Florian has served coffee under the arcades of Piazza San Marco since 1720, making it Italy's oldest café still in business. The mirrored, frescoed rooms are tiny jewel boxes, and on summer evenings a small orchestra plays on the square. Yes, the table price includes a music supplement. Pay it once; it's theatre.

Veneto guideCaffè Florian

Rome: wood-roasted beans and granita

Lazio

Sant'Eustachio, since 1938

Near the Pantheon, two roasters face off. Sant'Eustachio Il Caffè, open since 1938, still roasts over wood and whips its 'gran caffè' with sugar behind a screen so nobody can see how. Tazza d'Oro, roasting since 1944, is the place for granita di caffè con panna, coffee ice layered with whipped cream, on a hot afternoon.

Lazio guideTazza d'Oro

Padua: the café without doors

Veneto

Caffè Pedrocchi, since 1831

Caffè Pedrocchi opened in 1831 in a neoclassical palazzo and stayed open day and night for so long that it was nicknamed the café without doors. Its rooms are named by colour: red, white and green. Order the caffè Pedrocchi, espresso under a cool mint cream dusted with cocoa; the rule is no sugar and no stirring.

Veneto guideCaffè Pedrocchi

Milan: pastry and the Galleria

Lombardy

Marchesi, since 1824

Milan takes its coffee quickly and its pastry seriously. Pasticceria Marchesi has been baking since 1824, its original shop on Via Santa Maria alla Porta, with an elegant outpost in the Galleria. Across the arcade, Camparino in Galleria has poured Campari since 1915. Come at Christmas for panettone from the old pasticcerie.

Lombardy guidePasticceria Marchesi

Aperitivo hour

Between work and dinner, roughly 6pm to 8pm, Italy has a bitter, fizzy drink and a nibble. Here's what to order, and where it was born.

Spritz

Venice and Padua, Veneto

Aperol since 1919

The drink of the Veneto: prosecco, a bitter liqueur and a splash of soda, with an olive or an orange slice. Aperol was created in Padua in 1919; Venetians often prefer Select, born in Venice a year later, or a Campari spritz. In a Venetian bacaro it comes with cicchetti on the side.

Veneto guideAperol

Negroni

Florence, Tuscany

Born around 1919

Equal parts gin, Campari and red vermouth. The story goes that around 1919 Count Camillo Negroni asked the bartender at Florence's Caffè Casoni to stiffen his Americano with gin instead of soda. The bar, later Caffè Giacosa, reopened in 2023 in Via della Spada with a menu built around the drink.

Tuscany guide

Campari at the Camparino

Milan, Lombardy

Since 1915

Davide Campari opened the Camparino in 1915 at the entrance to the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, with a view of the Duomo. Stand at the Art Nouveau mosaic bar for a Campari seltz, chilled bitter shot with soda from a tap, the way Milanese have drunk it for a century.

Lombardy guideCamparino in Galleria

Vermouth di Torino

Turin, Piedmont

Since 1786

Turin gave the world vermouth: in 1786 Antonio Benedetto Carpano sold his aromatised wine near Piazza Castello, and the city's bars have mixed it ever since. Vermouth di Torino now has a protected geographical indication; order it neat over ice with a twist, alongside the city's generous aperitivo buffets.

Piedmont guideVermouth di Torino consortium

Gelato and granita

Two kinds of cold comfort, with very different rules.

How to spot good gelato

Everywhere

Look for covered tubs

Skip the towering, neon mounds. Good gelaterie keep their gelato in covered steel tubs (pozzetti) or low trays, in natural colours: pistachio should be olive-brown, banana greyish-white. Ask for a 'cono' or 'coppetta', choose two or three flavours, and say yes when they offer panna on top.

Sicilian granita

Sicily

With brioche, for breakfast

Sicily's summer breakfast: granita, coarse or creamy depending on the town, with a soft brioche col tuppo for dunking. Catania favours almond, pistachio and black mulberry; Messina swears by coffee with whipped cream. Have one in Noto at Caffè Sicilia, open since 1892.

Sicily guideCaffè Sicilia

Historic gelaterie

Rome and Florence

Giolitti since 1900

Giolitti, a short walk from the Pantheon, has scooped since 1900 under Belle Époque mirrors. In Florence, Vivoli in the Santa Croce quarter started as a dairy in 1929 and still serves its gelato in cups only. Florentines claim Bernardo Buontalenti invented gelato for the Medici; order the custard flavour named after him.

Lazio guideGiolitti

Carpigiani Gelato Museum

Anzola dell'Emilia, near Bologna

Booking required

A whole museum devoted to gelato, run by the company that builds the machines most Italian gelaterie use. Tour centuries of ice-making history, then make your own in a hands-on workshop. Book ahead.

Emilia-Romagna guideGelato Museum

Good to know

  • Most bars open early, around 6-7am, and many close by 8 or 9pm; Sunday hours are shorter.
  • At a grand café, check the table prices before you sit: they are always posted, but it's easy not to look.
  • Decaf is 'deca' or 'decaffeinato'; plant milk is increasingly common, so ask for 'latte di soia' or 'd'avena'.
  • Coffee to go (da portare via) is possible but uncommon; Italians drink it on the spot in a few seconds.
  • In Naples, the cuccuma (a flip-over coffee pot) and in the rest of Italy, the moka pot are how coffee is made at home: buy one as a souvenir.
