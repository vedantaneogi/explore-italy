Wheels, curds and caves: Italy's great cheeses

From Parmigiano dairies at dawn to buffalo farms near Paestum and cows high on summer pastures, here's where Italy's protected cheeses are made, and how to watch.

Towering shelves of Parmigiano Reggiano wheels ageing in a dairy near ModenaPhoto: Marie Thérèse Hébert & Jean Robert Thibault, CC BY-SA 2.0, via Wikimedia Commons

Italy has more than fifty cheeses with protected DOP status, and each one is tied to a place, a breed and a way of working that has barely changed in centuries. Parmigiano Reggiano is still made from raw milk in copper vats at dawn. Fontina comes from the milk of one alpine breed, milked once and made at once. Buffalo mozzarella is best eaten within hours of being pulled, at the farm, still warm.

That makes cheese one of the most rewarding things to travel for. Many consortia run visit programmes, dairies welcome early risers, and summer opens the high pastures, the alpeggi, where cheese is made in stone huts above the treeline. Below, the big names of the north, the south's fresh and sheep's-milk cheeses, and the places and festivals that let you see them made.

The north: big wheels and blue veins

Cow's-milk country, from the Po plain to the Alps. Ask for a taste at different ages; the difference is the whole point.

Parmigiano Reggiano

Parma, Reggio Emilia, Modena, Bologna, Mantua

DOP, aged 12 months+

Raw milk, rennet, salt and time: nothing else. Each 40 kg wheel ages at least 12 months, and the classic is 24 to 30, when it turns crumbly, crunchy with crystals and tastes of broth and nuts. Eat chunks broken with an almond-shaped knife and a drop of aged balsamic.

Emilia-Romagna guideVisit a dairy

Grana Padano

The Po plain, five regions

DOP, aged 9 months+

The other great grana, made across a wider swathe of northern Italy and the most produced DOP cheese in the world. Younger and milder than Parmigiano, it shines grated over risotto or shaved over carpaccio; the 'Riserva', aged over 20 months, is a match for its rival. Its heartland around Cremona also makes Provolone Valpadana, the stretched-curd cheese hung by ropes in shapes from small pears to giant logs.

Lombardy guideGrana Padano consortium

Gorgonzola

Piedmont and Lombardy

DOP, dolce or piccante

Named after a town east of Milan and now made mostly around Novara. Dolce is soft, creamy and spoonable; piccante is firmer, with dense blue-green veins and a real bite. Try it melted into gnocchi or with polenta, chestnut honey and a glass of sweet wine.

Lombardy guideGorgonzola consortium

Taleggio

Val Taleggio, Bergamo, Lombardy

DOP, washed rind

A square, washed-rind cheese first ripened in the caves of a steep valley above Bergamo. The thin pink-orange rind smells farmyard-strong, but the paste is mild, milky and buttery, with a hint of truffle. Lovely on polenta or melted in a risotto.

Lombardy guideTaleggio consortium

Fontina

Valle d'Aosta

DOP, raw milk

Made only from the raw milk of Valdostana cows, processed straight after each milking. Supple, sweet and nutty, it melts into fonduta, the valley's silky cheese fondue, often finished with white truffle. The prized Alpeggio version is made from June to September in summer pastures up to 2,700 metres.

Valle d'Aosta guideFontina alpeggi

Asiago

Asiago plateau, Veneto and Trentino

DOP, fresh or aged

Two cheeses under one name. Fresh Asiago Pressato is soft, mild and milky; Asiago d'Allevo is aged from a few months to two years until it turns grainy and sharp. Look for the 'Prodotto della Montagna' mark on cheese made entirely in the mountains.

Veneto guideAsiago consortium

Castelmagno

Valle Grana, Cuneo, Piedmont

DOP, mountain cheese

One of Italy's rarest cheeses, made in three mountain comuni of the Grana valley. Crumbly, slightly blue at the heart, savoury and long-lasting. The 'd'Alpeggio' label means it was made in summer above 1,000 metres. In Piedmont they melt it over gnocchi.

Piedmont guide

The south and the islands: fresh curds and sheep's milk

Buffalo and sheep take over as you head south. Fresh cheeses here are meant to be eaten the day they're made.

Mozzarella di Bufala Campana

Caserta and the Piana del Sele, Campania

DOP, eat same day

Made from the milk of water buffalo grazing the plains north of Naples and around Paestum. Good mozzarella should weep milk when cut, squeak slightly and never see a fridge. Eat it the day it's made, at room temperature, with nothing but a pinch of salt.

Campania guideMozzarella consortium

Burrata di Andria

Andria, Murgia, Puglia

IGP, eat within 24 hours

A pouch of fresh mozzarella filled with stracciatella, shreds of curd soaked in cream, traditionally tied with a green leaf. Born on a farm in the Murgia hills about a century ago, it is now an IGP. Buy it in Andria or Bari, and eat it within a day.

Puglia guideBurrata di Andria consortium

Pecorino Romano

Lazio, Sardinia and southern Tuscany

DOP, sheep's milk

The sharp, salty sheep's cheese that the legions ate on the march and Romans grate into carbonara and cacio e pepe today. Most is now made in Sardinia. On 1 May, Romans eat it in chunks with raw fava beans on a picnic.

Lazio guidePecorino Romano consortium

Pecorino Sardo

Sardinia

DOP, dolce or maturo

Sardinia keeps around three million sheep, about two for every islander, and makes two styles of its own pecorino: young, sweet and supple 'dolce', and smoky-edged, firm 'maturo'. Have it with carta da musica flatbread and a glass of Cannonau.

Sardinia guidePecorino Sardo consortium

Pecorino Toscano

Tuscany

DOP, fresh or aged

Softer and nuttier than its Roman cousin, sold fresh after a few weeks or aged for months. Pienza in the Val d'Orcia is the place to buy it, from shops that smell of hay and ash-rubbed rinds. Pair it with pears and honey, as the Tuscan saying goes.

Tuscany guidePecorino Toscano consortium

Ragusano

Hyblean plateau, Sicily

DOP, cow's milk

A huge brick-shaped stretched-curd cheese from the milk of cows, traditionally the red Modicana breed, grazing the stone-walled pastures above Ragusa and Modica. Hung in pairs over wooden beams to age, it turns sweet-savoury and spicy. Grate it over pasta or eat it with Nero d'Avola.

Sicily guide

See it made

Cheesemaking starts early. Most visits happen in the morning and need booking.

A Parmigiano Reggiano dairy at dawn

Parma, Reggio Emilia, Modena

Mornings, book ahead

Watch the cheesemaker break the curd with a spino, lift each twin mass from the copper vat in linen, then walk through cathedral-like ageing rooms stacked with thousands of wheels. The consortium's 'Vivi Parmigiano Reggiano' programme lists dairies that take visitors, with tastings of different ages. Twice a year, Caseifici Aperti weekends open dozens of dairies at once.

Emilia-Romagna guideVivi Parmigiano Reggiano

Tenuta Vannulo buffalo farm

Capaccio Paestum, Campania

Combine with Paestum

An organic buffalo farm near the Greek temples of Paestum where mozzarella is made by hand every day. Book a visit to see the buffalo and the dairy, then stay for buffalo-milk yogurt, gelato and the small museum of rural life.

Campania guideTenuta Vannulo

Musei del Cibo: Parmigiano Reggiano museum

Soragna, Emilia-Romagna

Weekends and holidays

Housed in a round nineteenth-century dairy building, the Parmigiano museum tells the story of the cheese with old tools, copper vats and a tasting. Pair it with the Prosciutto museum at Langhirano or the Culatello museum near the Po.

Emilia-Romagna guideMusei del Cibo

Fontina in the high pastures

Valle d'Aosta

June-September

From June to September, herds and cheesemakers move up to the alpeggi, and many huts sell cheese, butter and fresh ricotta direct to hikers. The Fontina consortium publishes a map of its summer pastures, a fine excuse for a mountain walk with a picnic at the end.

Valle d'Aosta guideMap of the alpeggi

Festivals and summer pastures

Cheese lovers should plan around these dates.

Cheese, Bra

Bra, Piedmont

September, odd years

Slow Food's biennial celebration of raw-milk cheese takes over the whole town of Bra, near the Langhe, with hundreds of cheesemakers from Italy and abroad, tastings, workshops and a market of mountain and shepherd cheeses. The 2025 edition ran in September; the next is expected in September 2027.

Piedmont guideCheese by Slow Food

Alpeggio: summer on the mountain

The Alps, from Piedmont to Friuli

June-September

The seasonal move of herds to high pastures is part of transhumance, listed by UNESCO as intangible heritage in 2019. Hike to a malga or alpeggio in July and August to see cheese made over a wood fire, and taste Bitto, Montasio, Puzzone di Moena or Fontina where they're born.

The descent of the herds

Valle d'Aosta, Alto Adige, Trentino

Late September-October

At the end of summer the cows come down from the pastures wearing flowers and bells: the Désarpa in Valle d'Aosta and the Almabtrieb in South Tyrol. Villages greet them with markets, music and, of course, cheese.

Caseifici Aperti

Parmigiano Reggiano area

Spring and autumn

Open-door weekends in spring and autumn when dozens of Parmigiano Reggiano dairies welcome visitors without booking, with tastings, farm lunches and demonstrations. Check the consortium site for the next dates.

Emilia-Romagna guideParmigiano Reggiano consortium

Good to know

  • Fresh cheeses like mozzarella and burrata are meant to be eaten the same day; buy them where they're made rather than flying them home.
  • Vacuum-packed hard cheeses travel well. Most dairies will vacuum-seal a wedge for you.
  • DOP (Denominazione di Origine Protetta) guarantees that a cheese was made in its home area to a strict recipe; look for the red and yellow seal.
  • In a cheese shop, ask for 'un assaggio' (a taste) and the age ('quanti mesi?'). Aged and fresh versions of the same cheese can taste like different foods.
  • Many Italians pair cheese with honey, mostarda or jam rather than with crackers.
