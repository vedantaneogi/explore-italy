Discover  /  Hidden gems

The Italy most visitors drive straight past

Hill villages, small islands, quiet coasts and handsome cities where you can still get a table without booking and hear more Italian than English.

Italy's famous places are famous for good reason, but they are a small slice of the country. Step one valley, one ferry or one train stop beyond the headline names and the pace changes: the bar owner remembers your order on day two, the lane to the church is empty at ten in the morning, and dinner costs what locals pay. None of the places below is a secret, exactly. They are simply loved mostly by Italians.

We have grouped them by mood rather than by region, and each card tells you why it is worth the detour and when it is at its best. Two quality marks are useful for finding more: I Borghi più Belli d'Italia, a club of more than 350 small historic villages, and the Touring Club Italiano's Bandiere Arancioni (orange flags), held by around 300 small inland towns for hospitality, heritage and care of the landscape.

Villages that time forgot

Stone villages on ridges and cliffs, some lovingly restored, one entirely abandoned. Stay the night if you can: they come alive when the day visitors leave.

Civita di Bagnoregio

Lazio, between Orvieto and Lake Bolsena

Why: a town on a vanishing rock · When: early morning, or misty October days

A medieval village on a pinnacle of crumbling tufa, reached only by a long, steep footbridge across the badlands of the Valle dei Calanchi. A handful of people still live here. There is a small entry fee; pay it, then linger in the lanes, the cellar wine bars and the belvedere over the clay gullies.

Lazio guide

Santo Stefano di Sessanio

Abruzzo, Gran Sasso National Park

Why: mountain silence and wool-trade stone · When: June to October

A fortified shepherds' village at about 1,250 metres, built by the wool trade and rescued from abandonment by careful restoration. Eat the tiny local lentils, walk up to the hilltop castle of Rocca Calascio, and drive across the vast Campo Imperatore plateau, nicknamed Italy's little Tibet.

Abruzzo guide

Craco

Basilicata, an hour from Matera

Why: Italy's most striking ghost town · When: spring or autumn, late afternoon light

A hilltop town emptied by landslides in the 1960s and left exactly as it was, its towers and palazzi slowly settling into the clay. You can visit only on a guided tour with a helmet, which makes it feel even more haunting. Film crews love it; you will see why.

Basilicata guide

Bosa

Sardinia, west coast

Why: colour, river life and sweet Malvasia · When: May to June, September

A sweep of pastel houses climbing from the River Temo to the Malaspina castle, with old tanneries along the water and a beach at Bosa Marina. Taste the amber Malvasia di Bosa, then drive the cliff road north towards Alghero, one of the island's loveliest.

Sardinia guide

Castelmezzano

Basilicata, Lucanian Dolomites

Why: village-on-the-rocks drama · When: May to September (zip line is seasonal)

Houses wedged between jagged sandstone spires, facing its twin village Pietrapertosa across the valley. Walk the Path of the Seven Stones between them, climb the via ferrata, or, if you dare, fly across on the Volo dell'Angelo, a steel-cable zip line high above the gorge.

Basilicata guideVolo dell'Angelo

Quiet islands

Skip the ferry queue to Capri and try one of these. Most are reached by hydrofoil or ferry in under two hours, and all reward a few nights rather than a day trip.

Procida

Campania, Bay of Naples

Why: real island life near Naples · When: May to June, September to October

The smallest island in the bay and the least polished, which is the point. Marina Corricella's ice-cream-coloured fishermen's houses, the fortified old town of Terra Murata, lemon gardens and a few small beaches. Ferries and hydrofoils from Naples take 40 to 60 minutes.

Campania guide

Favignana

Sicily, Egadi Islands

Why: bikes, coves and tuna history · When: June, September

A flat, butterfly-shaped island made for cycling: rent a bike at the port and pedal between turquoise coves like Cala Rossa and Bue Marino. The vast former Florio tuna cannery is now a superb museum. Hydrofoils from Trapani take about half an hour.

Sicily guide

Ustica

Sicily, north of Palermo

Why: some of Italy's clearest water · When: June to September

A small black volcanic island ringed by Italy's first marine protected area, created in 1986. Divers and snorkellers come for the clear water, grottoes and groupers; everyone else for the coastal path, the lighthouse and grilled fish on the harbour. Boats leave from Palermo.

Sicily guide

Pantelleria

Sicily, closer to Tunisia than to Palermo

Why: stark beauty, sweet wine · When: May to June, September to October

A wild volcanic island of black lava, low-domed stone dammusi, caper bushes and wind-sheltered zibibbo vines, whose head-trained growing method is UNESCO-listed. Bathe in the hot-spring-fed Lago di Venere and drink passito with almond biscuits. Fly from Palermo or Trapani.

Sicily guide

Asinara

Sardinia, off the north-west tip

Why: wild coast with a dark past · When: April to June, September

For a century a prison and quarantine island, now a national park with no private cars, white donkeys and empty beaches. Visit from Stintino or Porto Torres on a day trip, then explore by bike, electric car or guided tour. The old high-security prison is a sobering stop.

Sardinia guideAsinara National Park

Tremiti Islands

Puglia, off the Gargano coast

Why: Adriatic islands with Aegean water · When: June, September

Puglia's only archipelago: pine-scented San Domino with its coves and sea caves, and San Nicola, crowned by a fortified medieval abbey. The water is a protected marine reserve and gloriously clear. Ferries and fast boats run from Termoli and the Gargano ports, mostly in summer.

Puglia guide

Slow coasts

Seaside towns where the fishing boats are still working boats, and where the evening stroll matters more than the beach club.

Maratea

Basilicata, Tyrrhenian coast

Why: Amalfi-style cliffs without the crowds · When: June, September

Basilicata's only stretch of western coast: about 30 kilometres of cliffs, coves and black-sand beaches below a medieval hill town of many churches. A huge white statue of Christ the Redeemer stands on Monte San Biagio above it all. Intercity trains stop at Maratea station.

Basilicata guide

Scilla

Calabria, Strait of Messina

Why: myth, swordfish and water-lapped lanes · When: May to July

The rock of the sea monster in the Odyssey, now a castle above a fishing village. In the Chianalea quarter, houses rise straight out of the water and boats tie up at the front doors. In early summer, eat a swordfish sandwich made from the morning's catch.

Calabria guide

Camogli

Liguria, Riviera di Levante

Why: the Riviera at its most genuine · When: April to June, September to October

Tall painted houses crowd a pebble beach and a small harbour, just round the headland from glitzy Portofino. Walk or take the boat to the abbey of San Fruttuoso, eat focaccia on the sea wall, and time your visit for the giant-pan fish fry each May.

Liguria guide

Vieste

Puglia, Gargano peninsula

Why: beaches, caves and forest in one trip · When: June, September

A white old town on a limestone promontory above long sandy beaches and the lonely sea stack of Pizzomunno. Take a boat to the sea caves, see the wooden trabucchi fishing machines along the coast, and drive inland into the deep beech woods of the Foresta Umbra.

Puglia guide

Cala Gonone

Sardinia, Gulf of Orosei

Why: Sardinia's most dramatic coastline · When: late May to June, September

A small resort that is the gateway to the Gulf of Orosei, where limestone cliffs drop into impossibly blue water. Take a boat to Cala Luna or Cala Mariolu, explore the Bue Marino sea cave, or hike down to Cala Goloritzé, where visitor numbers are capped.

Sardinia guideCala Goloritzé info

Mountain and lake hideaways

The quieter cousins of Como and Cortina, with better hiking, cheaper rooms and nights so dark you can see the Milky Way.

Lake Orta

Piedmont, west of Lake Maggiore

Why: lake beauty at a whisper · When: April to June, September to October

A small, romantic lake with one perfect village, Orta San Giulio, and an island monastery where signs along the single lane ask you to walk in silence. Climb the Sacro Monte, a UNESCO-listed path of frescoed chapels, and take the little boat across at sunset.

Piedmont guide

Val di Funes

Trentino-Alto Adige, South Tyrol

Why: the Dolomites at their most pastoral · When: June to October

A farming valley below the sawtooth Odle (Geisler) peaks, home valley of the climber Reinhold Messner. The church of Santa Maddalena is famously photogenic; walk the Adolf Munkel trail beneath the peaks instead and stop at a mountain hut for dumplings and apple strudel.

Trentino-Alto Adige guide

Val Grande

Piedmont, above Lake Maggiore

Why: real wilderness near Milan · When: June to September

Italy's largest wilderness area, a national park of deep valleys and abandoned summer pastures that nature has reclaimed. There are no villages inside, just unstaffed huts and tough paths. For a gentler taste, walk from the edge villages above Verbania.

Piedmont guideVal Grande National Park

Lago di Braies, off-season

Trentino-Alto Adige, Dolomites

Why: a postcard without the queue · When: late September to October, January to February

This emerald lake under the cliffs of the Croda del Becco is mobbed in July and August, when car access works on a booking system. Come in late September or October for golden larches and an empty shoreline loop, or in deep winter, when the lake freezes and falls silent.

Trentino-Alto Adige guideAccess and booking

Bagni di Lucca

Tuscany, Serchio valley

Why: Tuscany's green, overlooked side · When: May to October

A faded spa town in wooded hills where Shelley, Byron and the Brownings came to take the waters. Cross the 19th-century chain bridge, soak in the thermal caves, and visit the humpbacked Devil's Bridge at nearby Borgo a Mozzano. Lucca is under an hour away by train.

Tuscany guide

Cities without the crowds

Handsome, lively, well-fed cities that most itineraries skip. Each makes a better base than you would think.

Trieste

Friuli Venezia Giulia, on the Slovenian border

Why: coffee, sea air and Mitteleuropa · When: May to October

A Habsburg port city where Italian, Slovene and Austrian worlds meet. Piazza Unità d'Italia opens straight onto the sea, the old coffee houses are where James Joyce wrote, and locals sunbathe on the Barcola promenade. In season, seek out an osmiza, a Karst farmhouse selling its own wine.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia guideDiscover Trieste

Lecce

Puglia, Salento

Why: the Baroque capital of the south · When: April to June, September to October

A honey-coloured city of riotous Baroque carved from soft local limestone, with a Roman amphitheatre half-buried in its main square. Start the day with a warm pasticciotto custard pastry, browse the papier-mâché workshops, and use it as a base for the Salento beaches.

Puglia guide

Mantua

Lombardy, on three lakes

Why: Renaissance art without the queues · When: April to June, September to October

The Gonzaga dukes' city, ringed by lakes formed from the River Mincio. Inside the vast Palazzo Ducale is Mantegna's painted Bridal Chamber; across town, Giulio Romano's Palazzo Te is pure Renaissance fun. Eat tortelli di zucca, pumpkin pasta with a hint of mostarda.

Lombardy guideMantua tourism

Urbino

Marche, on a ridge in the Montefeltro hills

Why: a whole Renaissance city on a hill · When: April to June, September to October

Raphael's home town and the ideal Renaissance court, built around Duke Federico da Montefeltro's palace. Its National Gallery of the Marche holds Piero della Francesca's enigmatic Flagellation. The university keeps the steep brick streets young and the bars cheap.

Marche guide

Ascoli Piceno

Marche, near the Abruzzo border

Why: a travertine jewel box · When: May to October

A city built almost entirely of pale travertine, whose Piazza del Popolo is one of Italy's most beautiful squares. Order an anisette at Caffè Meletti, eat olive all'ascolana, crisp fried stuffed olives, and come in summer for the Quintana jousting tournament.

Marche guide

Bergamo

Lombardy, an hour from Milan

Why: a hilltop old town next to the airport · When: year-round

Most people only see Bergamo's airport. Take the funicular up to the Città Alta instead: a medieval upper town wrapped in UNESCO-listed Venetian walls, with Piazza Vecchia at its heart. Stay for casoncelli, butter-and-sage stuffed pasta, and a walk along the ramparts at dusk.

Lombardy guideVisit Bergamo

Ferrara

Emilia-Romagna, between Bologna and Venice

Why: Italy's best city by bike · When: April to October

The Este family's Renaissance city, planned with wide streets and centred on a moated castle. Locals go everywhere by bike, and so should you: the nine kilometres of city walls make a perfect green ring to ride. Try cappellacci di zucca with butter and sage.

Emilia-Romagna guideFerrara tourism

Good to know

  • Stay at least one night in a village: the lanes empty by late afternoon and you will have them to yourself.
  • Many small islands and coastal towns run reduced ferries, and close hotels and restaurants, from November to March, so check timetables before you plan.
  • Rural Italy is much easier with a car; where we suggest a train or ferry instead, we have said so.
  • Look for the Borghi più Belli d'Italia and Bandiere Arancioni signs at village entrances: they are a reliable guide to well-kept places.
  • In August, Italians head to the coast and the mountains too. For quiet, aim for June or September.
